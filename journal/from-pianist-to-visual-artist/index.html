From Pianist to Visual Artist: My Creative Evolution

Before the Canvas

I grew up as a pianist. Not as someone who took piano lessons, but as someone whose life was structured around the instrument. Practice, performance, the constant calibration of technique and feeling. I'm sometimes asked when I decided to become a visual artist. The honest answer: I didn't decide. I evolved. The shift from piano to visual art wasn't rejection but translation. Both are about the same fundamental thing: translating internal experience into external form through material mastery.

What Piano Taught Me

Discipline and precision: Piano requires exactly that. If you play an F-sharp when you meant to play F, the note is wrong. There's no approximation. This taught me that technical precision matters—not as an end in itself, but as the foundation for expression. When people see my geometric compositions, they sometimes assume I value precision above all. But what piano taught me is that precision is how you earn the right to imprecision. Technical mastery gives you the freedom to break rules deliberately.

The relationship between structure and spontaneity: A musical piece has structure—rhythm, form, predetermined sequences. But the performance itself is spontaneous. Two pianists playing the same composition never sound identical. Feeling, intuition, the specific moment determine how notes are voiced, how fast they're played, what emphasis each phrase receives. This is exactly the relationship I'm trying to achieve in my visual work. Structure (the geometric framework) combined with spontaneity (the fluid pouring of acrylics). Both are essential. Neither alone is sufficient.

Listening: This is the piano skill most people don't recognize. You play notes, yes, but you're also constantly listening—to whether this phrase worked, whether the balance between melody and accompaniment is right, whether the pacing serves the emotional content. This translated directly into visual practice. I learned to "listen" to my compositions—to watch how colors interact, whether something's off balance, whether a section needs more emphasis or should recede. It's the same skill, applied to visual rather than auditory material.

When Piano Stopped Being Enough

I loved playing piano. But around high school, I started feeling that something was missing. Piano is constrained by the instrument. The keys are fixed. The possible sounds are predetermined. I wanted to work with material that could be more open-ended. That wasn't bound by a single instrument's limitations. I wanted to reclaim my own story and decide how to tell it. This might sound dramatic, but it's true. Piano has a centuries-long tradition, established technique, conventional expectations about what the instrument can do. Those traditions are beautiful and constraining simultaneously. Visual art, especially abstraction, felt more open. I could invent my own methodology. I could use any materials I wanted. I could fail in new ways. So I started painting alongside my music training. For a while, I did both. But increasingly, visual art drew my attention. I was fascinated by how abstract forms could communicate without representation—how color relationships could carry meaning without depicting anything specific.

The Parallel Practice Phase

For several years, I was both pianist and visual artist. This wasn't conflict—it was enrichment. The disciplines informed each other constantly. When I was stuck on a visual problem, piano practice would offer unexpected solutions. A musical composition's approach to building tension over time informed how I thought about building visual tension in a painting. Conversely, visual thinking changed how I heard music. I became more attentive to structure, to how a piece was organized spatially (in time). I noticed rhythm as visual pattern, not just temporal sequence. Some of my best discoveries came from this cross-pollination. I learned that both disciplines are really about the same thing: organizing material to create meaning and emotion.

Why I Eventually Chose Visual Art

This wasn't about rejecting piano. It was about recognizing that visual art could do something I'd been searching for: create space for others to find their own meaning. Piano is partly about interpretation, yes, but the composer's intent is still primary. When you perform a Bach prelude, you're interpreting Bach's ideas, not your own. Your individuality is expressed through nuance within predetermined structure. But abstract visual art is different. The viewer isn't receiving pre-determined meaning to be interpreted. They're encountering visual structures and finding meaning through their own engagement. I wanted to create that kind of space. Where meaning wasn't mine to impose but emerged through the viewer's encounter with the work.

How Piano Still Shapes My Art

Even though I'm no longer primarily a pianist, piano training remains fundamental to my practice: technique as foundation, composition and structure, rhythm and pacing, and the dialogue between intention and material. All of these come directly from music training.
